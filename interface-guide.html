A beginner visual lesson

How to read a website interface

A website interface is everything people see and use on a page. First notice its big regions; then ask what each region helps a visitor do.

Read from top to bottom: identity and navigation → main message → supporting choices → helpful details.

1. The main parts

Header
Usually at the top. Shows the site name or logo and useful account links.
Navigation
Menus and links that help people move between pages. Labels should be clear.
Main content
The reason the page exists: an article, product, service, form, or tool.
Hero area
The first prominent section. It often has a headline, short explanation, and key action.
Call to action (CTA)
A button or link that makes a next step clear, such as “Book a visit.”
Footer
The bottom area, often containing contact details, policies, and secondary links.

2. How visual design guides attention

Hierarchy means making the most important item easiest to notice. Larger headings, stronger contrast, and a clear primary button help establish order.

Layout and spacing group related things and leave breathing room. Alignment gives elements a tidy edge. Color can show emphasis or state, but should not be the only way information is conveyed.

Typography is the choice and styling of text. Use readable sizes, clear heading levels, and short paragraphs. Images and icons should explain or support content, not distract from it.

3. How people interact

Links
Take you somewhere else. They should look and read like things you can follow.
Buttons
Perform an action, such as saving, submitting, or opening something.
Forms
Collect information. Each field needs a label; errors should say how to fix them.
Feedback
After an action, the page should show what happened: success, loading, or a useful error.

4. Three qualities of a good interface

Usability: Can someone complete their task easily, without guessing?

Accessibility: Can people with different abilities use it? Think keyboard access, readable contrast, text alternatives for meaningful images, and labels for controls.

Responsive design: Does it adapt to different screen sizes? On phones, columns may stack, text stays readable, and controls remain easy to tap.

5. A simple way to review any page

  1. Who is this page for, and what do they want to do?
  2. Can you tell what the page offers from its headline?
  3. Is the next action obvious?
  4. Can you find your way around and understand each control?
  5. Check it at phone size and try using only the keyboard.

Remember: a good interface helps people understand where they are, what they can do, and what happens next.

Observe → Understand → Try → Improve
